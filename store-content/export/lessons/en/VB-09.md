# 1. Lesson identity
**Full title:** Volleyball 09 — 3v3 Play and Role Transition
**Lesson code:** VB-09-EN
**Sport:** Volleyball
**Subtopic:** basic rotation and decisions
**Age:** 13–16
**Experience:** intermediate
**Duration:** 45 minutes
**Group:** 20–30 students of all genders; adapt by ability and need
**Equipment:** 12–16 balls, net or rope, 30 cones, bibs
**Space:** gym or flat court with four small courts
**Complexity:** intermediate
# 2. Overall purpose
Keep a triangle, transition from reception to attack, and rotate in order.
# 3. Observable learning objectives
- Holds a triangle shape with two team-mates at the moment the ball crosses the net, in 4 of 5 rallies.
- Transitions from the receiving position to an attacking position after the first contact, in 3 of 4 rallies.
- Rotates in the correct order at each change of service, without needing a reminder from the teacher.
- Calls before every contact, so that no ball is played by two students or left by both.
- Takes receiving, setting, and attacking roles across the rotation and names one factual cue about a partner's transition.
# 4. Required prior knowledge
VB-08 — net defence and coverage.
# 5. Position in the learning pathway
**Previous lesson:** VB-08 — net defence and coverage.  
**Current lesson:** It removes the fixed jobs the pathway has relied on: students rotate through every role, so the same person receives in one rally and sets in the next, and must transition from defending to attacking inside a single rally. It withholds formal assessment — the teacher still coaches during play — which VB-10 replaces with observation against criteria.  
**Next lesson:** VB-10 — final game and assessment.
# 6. Lesson contribution
**Motor:** coordination, posture, timing, accuracy. **Social:** communication and turns. **Cognitive:** reading flight and deciding. **Tactical:** support and coverage. **Emotional:** confidence and recovery.
# 7. Equipment and space
Four 8×4 m courts with adapted net/rope. Spare balls secured. Setup 6 minutes; pack-down 4; groups of 5–8 rotate every 60–90 seconds.
# 8. Safety
- Check surface, net anchors, and balls.
- Never hang on or cross under the net.
- Keep two arm lengths and stay out of adjacent courts.
- Enter the ball path only after calling.
- Retrieve loose balls after scanning.
- Use a soft ball and low net when needed.
- Stop for pain, dizziness, or collision.
# 9. Lesson sequence
## Opening
**Duration:** 3 minutes.  
**Teacher says:** “From today nobody has one job. You receive, you set, you attack, and you rotate — which means every one of you has to be able to do all three. Keep the triangle and call every ball.”  
**Purpose:** state the cues — triangle, three calls, transition after the send — plus the rotation order and the rule that rotation happens only between rallies.  
**Organisation:** class in a half-circle facing a court with the rotation order marked.
## Warm-up
**Duration:** 7 minutes.  
**Purpose:** prepare the whole body for continuous rallies and rehearse changing role while moving.  
**Structure:** individual movement, then trios.  
**Instructions:** jog with direction changes on a colour call; add arm swings, side steps, and low shuffles; then in trios play a continuous triangle of contacts in which every student must move to a different point after each touch.  
**Teaching points:** your next position is decided by where you just played the ball from; call before you touch it.  
**Transitions:** move to courts on the colour signal.  
**Variations:** foundation — catch, call, and throw; advanced — alternate forearm and overhead contacts.
## Main activity A
**Duration:** 10 minutes.  
**Purpose:** learn the rotation order well enough that it costs no attention during a rally.  
**Procedure:** trios play a simple receive–set–send sequence, rotate one position on the teacher's signal, and repeat from their new roles. Work 40 seconds, then 20 seconds of feedback, repeated four times.  
**Organisation:** one trio per court with the three positions and the rotation direction marked on the floor.  
**Teaching points:** rotate only between rallies, never while the ball is live; know who you follow and who follows you; every position has a base, so find it before the next ball.  
**Common errors:** rotating mid-rally; students quietly staying in their favourite role; forgetting the base position of a newly taken role; the strongest player taking the setting role every time.
## Main activity B
**Duration:** 10 minutes.  
**Purpose:** keep communication and shape intact once two teams are competing.  
**Procedure:** 3v3 with the rule that no contact counts unless the player called it first; an uncalled contact simply restarts the rally. Roles rotate every minute.  
**Organisation:** two trios per court with an observer listening for calls and reporting how many contacts were called.  
**Teaching points:** transition the moment the ball leaves your side, because defending shape and attacking shape are different; whoever received should not also be the one who attacks; keep the triangle.  
**Common errors:** silence once the rally speeds up; receiving and then standing still; the trio flattening into a line; two players calling at once.
## Applied game
**Duration:** 10 minutes.  
**Purpose:** play the full small-sided game with rotation, transition, and communication all running at once.  
**Rules:** games to five points with full rotation at each change of service; one point for winning the rally plus a bonus point for any rally in which the team used three contacts and rotated correctly. No spiking; attacks from the floor only.  
**Scoring:** one point for execution plus a decision bonus; no individual ranking.  
**Teaching points:** short games mean everyone passes through every role; the bonus rewards structure, not the result.  
**Common errors:** abandoning rotation to keep the best player at the net; dropping to one contact under pressure; arguments over whose ball it was, which the calling rule exists to settle.
## Closure
**Duration:** 5 minutes.  
**Key message:** “A team where everyone can receive, set, and attack is far harder to play against than one with a single good player — that is what the rotation is teaching you.”  
**Learning summary:** each student names one piece of evidence about a role they found difficult, plus one next step.  
**Feedback:** partners exchange one observed fact and one suggestion.  
**Orderly exit:** balls collected by zone, then a head count.
# 10. Common errors
- **Contact before position:** ball behind body; use “feet before hands.”
- **Eyes only on ball:** collision risk; call and scan.
- Rotating while the ball is live: loss of control; slow down and reset cues.
- **Power over direction:** ball travels long; near target and 60% force.
- **Standing after contact:** court uncovered; move to support.
- **Vague feedback:** give one fact and one suggestion.
# 11. Teaching points
- Demonstrate front and side, normal then slow.
- Teach body path before contact.
- Pair visual and sound cues.
- Increase distance after three successes.
- Observe one learner for 45–60 seconds.
- Protect equal movement time.
# 12. Adaptations
**Foundation:** catch–throw, soft ball, low net. **Intermediate:** continuous contact. **Advanced:** small target or short time. **Large group:** six courts. **Small group:** 2v2. **Limited space:** short court. **Partial equipment:** one ball per trio and rope. **Varied needs:** visual cue, strong markings, extra time, fixed partner.
# 13. Assessment
**Checks:** triangle shape, transition from receiving to attacking, rotation order, calling, communication. **Success:** 4–5 criteria. **Partial:** 2–3 with prompt. **Not yet:** 0–1 or unsafe; return to soft-ball catch–throw.
# 14. Reflection
**Teacher:** Who acted independently? Did everyone contact the ball? Students: What did you see and where did you move? Next: revisit one criterion.
# 15. Continuity
**If met:** VB-10 — final game and assessment. **If not:** return to catch, shorten distance, and lower net.

# 19. Pedagogical value
**Why important?** Rotation is what makes volleyball an all-round sport: because every player must pass through every position, no student can hide in one role, and no team can be carried by a single specialist.  
**What develops?** The ability to switch role mid-rally, shape awareness that survives fatigue, communication precise enough to prevent collisions, and competence in the two or three roles each student would otherwise avoid.  
**Why not skip?** Without rotation the strongest student takes every ball and the rest spectate, which is exactly the pattern that makes school volleyball unpopular; it is also the point where students who are weaker at one skill discover they are stronger at another.
